Graphic-Based PMP Questions: The 2026 Exam Format Explained

August 17, 2026 7 min read Exam Format

The 2026 PMP exam introduced a question type that catches text-only studiers off guard: the graphic-based question. PMI now hands you a chart, a graph, or a diagram and asks you to read it to answer. It is one of only two brand-new question types PMI added for July 2026, and unlike some of the others, it shows up whether you test at a center or online. If your prep is all reading, you have a blind spot. This post explains what these questions are, where they come from, and how to practice the one skill they test.

What PMI actually says

This is not a rumor from a prep forum. PMI's official July 2026 Examination Content Outline lists the question types on the exam, and two of them are flagged NEW. One is the case or scenario question. The other is the graphic-based question.

PMI defines a graphic-based question as one that requires the candidate to refer to and interpret visual information, such as charts, graphs, diagrams, or images, then answer based on the information provided. The outline also notes it is available on all modalities, meaning both the computer-based test at a Pearson VUE center and the online proctored exam. That last detail matters. Some of the other new interactions, like point-and-click hotspots and enhanced matching, are computer-based only. Graphic-based questions are on every version of the exam.

So every 2026 candidate will face them. The only question is whether you have practiced reading a chart under time pressure before you sit down.

What a graphic-based question looks like

Picture a sprint burndown chart. The x-axis is days in the sprint, the y-axis is story points remaining, and there are two lines: the ideal burndown running straight to zero, and the actual work line tracking above it. The question is not "what is a burndown chart." The question is something like: "Based on the chart, what should the Scrum Master address first?" To answer, you have to see that the actual line is flat for three days in the middle of the sprint, read that as stalled progress, and pick the response that fits.

The chart is the question. There is no paragraph restating what the chart shows. If you cannot read the visual, you cannot answer, and no amount of memorized definitions rescues you. That is the shift. The 2026 exam is testing whether you can pull a decision out of a project artifact the way a working project manager does, not whether you can recite what the artifact is called.

Common visuals to expect: burndown and burnup charts, earned value graphs (the three-curve PV, EV, AC picture), network diagrams and critical paths, risk matrices, cost and schedule performance trends, and stakeholder or RACI grids. Anything a project manager reads on the job is fair game as something you read on the exam.

Why this is harder than it sounds

Reading a chart is easy when you have unlimited time and the chart is the only thing in front of you. On the exam you have roughly 80 seconds per question, the chart may have five data series when the question turns on one, and the answer choices are written to reward a fast wrong reading.

The trap is the same one that catches people on text questions: you glance at the visual, pattern-match to something familiar, and pick before you have found what the question is actually asking about. On a graphic-based question that means grabbing the wrong data point. The fix is a deliberate read order, look at the question stem first, find out which single value or trend it depends on, then go to the chart and find only that. The rest of the chart is noise the item writer put there on purpose.

This is the same read-the-ask-first discipline that works on scenario questions, applied to a picture instead of a paragraph. If you want the full version of that method, we break it down in how to read a PMP practice question.

Where graphic-based questions fit in the 2026 changes

Graphic-based questions did not arrive alone. The July 2026 overhaul re-weighted the domains, moved the body of knowledge to PMBOK 8, and added the case-study format. The new question types are one piece of a broader push to make the exam feel less like a vocabulary test and more like the job.

Here is how the two genuinely new question types compare.

A detailed scenario, sometimes with a chart for context, followed by several linked questions. Usually one question where the visual itself is the thing you must read to answer.
The visual is background; the words carry most of the information. The visual carries the information; there is little or no restating text.

How to prepare for them

The preparation is simple to state and easy to skip: practice on questions that actually show you a chart. Reading a blog paragraph about burndown charts is not the same as being handed one and asked to make a call in 80 seconds. The muscle you need is visual, and you only build it by using it.

Three habits that carry over from strong candidates:

Read the question before the chart. Find the single value or trend the item depends on, then go looking for just that. Do not study the whole chart first.

Name the artifact. The second you see the visual, identify it: burndown, earned value, network diagram, risk matrix. Knowing the type tells you where the answer usually hides.

Ignore the decoy data. Item writers add extra series and labels to pull your eye. If the question is about schedule variance, the cost line is noise. Read past it.

Common Questions

Does the 2026 PMP exam really have graphic-based questions?
Yes. PMI's July 2026 Exam Content Outline lists Graphic-Based Questions as a new question type, defined as questions that require you to refer to and interpret visual information such as charts, graphs, diagrams, or images, then answer based on that information. They are available on all exam modalities, both computer-based testing at a center and online proctored.

What is the difference between a case study and a graphic-based question?
A case or scenario question presents a detailed situation, which may include graphs or charts for context, followed by a series of linked questions. A graphic-based question is usually a single question where the visual is the thing you have to read to answer. Both are new in 2026, but the graphic-based type puts the chart at the center of one question rather than as background for several.

Are graphic-based questions on both the in-person and online PMP exam?
Yes. PMI lists graphic-based questions as available on all modalities, so you will see them whether you take the exam at a test center or online with a proctor. Some other new formats, such as point-and-click hotspot questions and enhanced matching, are only on the computer-based test.

How do I practice for graphic-based PMP questions?
Practice on questions that actually put a chart or diagram in front of you, not just text about charts. The skill is reading the visual first, finding the one value or trend the question turns on, and ignoring the rest.
